What each JPEG quality level actually weighs

We encoded one 1200×630 image (44,915 bytes as PNG) at eight quality settings in headless Chrome 149 and recorded the resulting Blob.size. These are measurements, not estimates. The last two columns decode each result back and compare it with the untouched source pixels, so the size saving sits next to what it actually costs:

The default of 0.75 is not a compression setting. At that value the output is still 30% larger than the source file it came from. That source only starts shrinking below roughly 0.57 — so if a tool shows you “quality 75%” and a number that went up, this is why.

The cheap savings are over by 0.92. Going from 0.92 down to 0.75 removes 35% of the bytes (90,251 → 58,265) but doubles the average pixel error (0.98 → 1.99 levels out of 255) and nearly triples the share of pixels that are off by more than 8 levels (1.9% → 5.4%). Below 0.92 you are no longer trimming overhead, you are trading picture for size.

Re-encoding is front-loaded, not compounding. We took the same source, encoded it at 0.92, then decoded and re-encoded it four more times, measuring every generation against the original pixels:

Four extra generations cost 20% more error on top of the first encode (0.98 → 1.18 levels), 2.7 dB of PSNR, and 1.0% of the file size — and each pass damages less than the one before it (0.98, then 0.16, 0.10, 0.08, 0.06). The original encode at 0.92 is what costs you the picture; the re-saves mostly cost you the archive. Compress once, from the best source you have, and keep that source.

⚠️ Two honest boundaries. On this image the mean error at 0.75 (1.99) is higher than at 0.60 (1.89) while the tail gets worse (pixels off by more than 8 levels: 5.4% → 6.6%) — a single average is not a verdict, because the encoder's quantisation tables do not scale evenly between steps. And all of it was measured on one flat-colour graphic; photographs with fine texture land on different numbers, which is what the next section measures.

WebP is smaller than JPEG at every one of those settings — the same image at 0.75 is 22,212 B. Compare the two on the JPG to WebP tool before picking a format to ship.

At the same setting a photograph pays about twice the error. At 0.92 its average pixel moves 1.92 levels where the graphic moves 0.98, and the tail is worse than the average admits: push both to 0.75 and 22.1% of the photograph's pixels are off by more than 8 levels — one pixel in five — against 5.4% for the graphic. Advice calibrated on logos (“75% is fine”) does not survive the trip to a photo. On this photograph, 0.85 is the last setting that keeps the tail under roughly 14%.

A photograph also has more to give away, which is why the slider feels generous. From 0.92 to 0.75 it loses 44.4% of its bytes against 35.4% for the graphic, and by 0.10 it is down to 3.3% of its own 1.00 size while the graphic stops at 7.4% — texture is redundancy. The catch is where the damage lands: that same 0.92 → 0.75 step multiplies the tail 5.2× on the photograph and 2.8× on the graphic.

WebP's advantage halves on a photograph. JPEG 0.92 to WebP 0.85 saves 69.2% on the graphic and 35.8% on the photo. It is still the better encoder — WebP 0.85 holds the photograph's error at 2.39 against JPEG 0.85's 2.85 while writing 14% fewer bytes — but a size budget built on “WebP saves 60%” is a budget built on a logo. Halve it for photographs.

⚠️ One measurement trap, recorded because it nearly shipped. The first pass used a JPEG as its source, and at 0.75 the error dropped to 0.12 levels — re-encoding an image at the quality it was originally saved at reproduces its own quantisation table almost exactly, so the step looks free. The photograph here is therefore a lossless 1200×800 re-save and every number above is a first-generation encode. The side effect: “re-save at the same quality” is measurable rather than mythical — we fell into it by accident.
